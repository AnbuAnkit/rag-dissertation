Abstract
Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) models have become crucial in healthcare applications, significantly 
enhancing the relevance and reliability of AI-driven insights by combining the generative capabilities of large 
language models (LLMs) with retrieval-based methods. As healthcare data demand precision and accountability, 
RAG models address critical limitations of LLMs, such as the tendency to “hallucinate”or produce inaccurate 
information—by incorporating real-time retrieval from trusted medical knowledge bases and clinical literature. 
This dual process of retrieving and generating ensures that responses are both contextually accurate and aligned 
with the latest clinical evidence, making RAG models especially valuable for medical question answering, 
diagnostics, and treatment planning. This paper presents an in-depth exploration of RAG models and LLMs, 
specifically within healthcare contexts, to meet the unique demands of medical data processing and decision 
support. It reviews various RAG architectures, including Naive, Advanced, and Modular RAG approaches, 
discussing how each framework optimizes retrieval depth, response quality, and computational efficiency. By 
reducing errors in patient-care recommendations, RAG models play an essential role in scenarios that require high 
precision and accountability. Additionally, this survey addresses the ethical considerations and transparency 
requirements for deploying RAG models in healthcare, identifying current challenges and future directions, such 
as enhancing source interpretability and adapting RAG frameworks for specialized medical fields. By system- 
atically analyzing RAG techniques, this paper provides a comprehensive guide to the state-of-the-art in RAG 
applications within healthcare, positioning RAG models as a transformative tool for advancing AI-supported 
healthcare outcomes.

1 Introduction

The rapid advancement of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly in natural language processing, has ushered in a 
new era of possibilities for healthcare. Large language models (LLMs) have emerged as transformative tools, 
demonstrating remarkable capabilities in understanding, generating, and reasoning with medical language [1, 2]. 
From general-purpose models like GPT-3 and GPT-4 to specialized medical models such as Med-PaLM [3], 
these AI systems are reshaping clinical practice by enhancing diagnostic accuracy, improving patient-provider 
communication, and supporting personalized treatment planning.
  However, the application of LLMs in healthcare presents unique challenges that demand specialized solutions. 
The high-stakes nature of medical decision-making, where errors can have life-altering consequences, requires an

unprecedented level of accuracy and reliability. Traditional LLMs, while powerful, face several critical limita- 
tions in healthcare settings:
• Static Knowledge Problem: Medical knowledge evolves rapidly, with new research, clinical guidelines, and 
  treatment protocols emerging constantly [4]. LLMs trained on fixed datasets quickly become outdated, 
  potentially providing recommendations based on superseded information.
• Hallucination Risk: The tendency of LLMs to generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information 
  is particularly dangerous in healthcare contexts, where accuracy is paramount [5].
• Domain-Specific Nuances: Medical language contains specialized terminology, abbreviations, and contextual 
  dependencies that general-purpose LLMs may misinterpret [6].
• Evidence-Based Practice: Modern medicine requires decisions to be grounded in verifiable evidence, yet 
  standard LLMs often lack transparent sourcing for their outputs [7].
To address these challenges, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) models have emerged as a promising 
solution [8]. RAG architectures combine the generative capabilities of LLMs with dynamic retrieval from 
authoritative medical knowledge bases, creating systems that can:
• Access up-to-date clinical guidelines and research findings
• Ground responses in verifiable sources
• Adapt to specialized medical subdomains
• Provide transparent rationales for clinical recommendations
The potential applications of RAG in healthcare are vast and transformative:
  Clinical Decision Support: RAG systems can assist clinicians by retrieving relevant case studies, treatment 
guidelines, and drug interaction information in real-time, reducing diagnostic errors and improving treatment 
outcomes [9].
 Medical Education: These models can serve as intelligent tutors, providing medical students with accurate, 
context-aware explanations drawn from the latest literature [10].
 Patient Engagement: By generating personalized health information grounded in authoritative sources, RAG 
systems can improve health literacy and shared decision-making [11].
 Administrative Efficiency: Automating documentation tasks like clinical note generation and coding with 
retrieval-verified accuracy can reduce clinician burnout [12].
 This paper provides a comprehensive examination of RAG models in healthcare, analyzing their architectural 
variations, performance characteristics, and practical implementations. We systematically review:
1. The evolution of RAG architectures from naive to advanced modular implementations
2. Performance benchmarks across medical specialties and use cases
3. Ethical considerations and implementation challenges
4. Emerging applications and future directions
Our analysis reveals that while RAG models show tremendous promise in healthcare applications [13], their 
successful implementation requires careful consideration of:
• Retrieval accuracy and relevance in specialized medical contexts
• Integration with clinical workflows and electronic health record systems
• Privacy and security protections for sensitive patient data
• Regulatory compliance and liability frameworks
• Bias mitigation and equity considerations
As healthcare systems worldwide face increasing pressure to deliver high-quality, cost-effective care, RAG 
models offer a pathway to augment clinical expertise with AI-powered knowledge retrieval and synthesis [14]. 
This review aims to provide researchers, clinicians, and healthcare administrators with the insights needed to 
harness this potential responsibly and effectively.

1.1 Contributions

This survey paper makes the following key contributions:
• Systematic Taxonomy of RAG Architectures: A hierarchical classification of Naive, Advanced, and 
  Modular RAG frameworks with comparative analysis of their components and performance characteristics in 
  medical applications.
• Data-to-Model Pipeline Analysis: Comprehensive examination of the medical AI pipeline from foundational 
  datasets (textual, visual, and multimodal) to implemented systems, highlighting distinct challenges and 
  solutions for different data modalities.
• Medical Application Spectrum: Coverage of RAG implementations across clinical domains including 
  diagnostic support, treatment planning, medical education, and multimodal interpretation of imaging data.
• Rigorous Model Evaluation: Standardized assessment of RAG systems using both quantitative metrics 
  (accuracy, BLEU, ROUGE) and qualitative frameworks (S.C.O.R.E.) across medical benchmarks.
• Implementation Challenges: Critical analysis of deployment barriers including data privacy, algorithmic 
  bias, regulatory requirements, and clinical workflow integration.
• Emerging Research Frontiers: Identification of promising directions including real-time knowledge 
  integration, specialty-specific architectures, and validation protocols for clinical adoption.
This work establishes a foundational reference for AI researchers, medical practitioners, and healthcare tech- 
nologists developing next-generation RAG systems for medicine.


2 Comprehensive applications of retrieval-augmented generation 
  in healthcare [15–19]

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) models represent a transformative advancement in healthcare AI by 
synergizing the generative capabilities of Large Language Models (LLMs) with real-time access to authoritative 
medical knowledge sources. This integration addresses critical challenges such as outdated training data and the 
propensity for AI hallucination, ensuring that outputs are both accurate and grounded in the latest clinical 
evidence. The following subsections explore the diverse applications of RAG in the medical domain, highlighting 
its impact on clinical workflows, diagnostic precision, and patient care.

2.1 Enhanced clinical documentation and decision support

RAG models excel in processing and summarizing vast volumes of clinical data, including electronic health 
records (EHRs), research literature, and patient notes. By distilling this information into concise summaries, they 
empower healthcare professionals to quickly grasp complex cases. For instance, RAG-powered question-an- 
swering (QA) systems retrieve evidence-based responses from trusted repositories like PubMed or clinical 
guidelines, enabling clinicians to make informed decisions without manually sifting through extensive databases.
 Key applications include:

• Automated Clinical Summarization: Generation of patient summaries by synthesizing information from 
  multiple EHR sections, laboratory results, and imaging reports
• Real-time Clinical Guidelines Retrieval: Dynamic access to updated treatment protocols and best practices 
  during patient encounters
• Medication Reconciliation: Cross-referencing patient medications with drug interaction databases and 
  formulary guidelines
• Discharge Planning: Automated generation of discharge summaries with personalized patient instructions 
  based on diagnosis and treatment history
This capability is particularly valuable in time-sensitive scenarios, such as emergency medicine or ICU care, 
where rapid access to relevant clinical information can significantly impact patient outcomes.

2.2 Precision in named entity recognition (NER) and clinical data extraction

In healthcare, extracting structured information from unstructured text—such as diagnoses, medications, or 
laboratory results—is essential for automating workflows and maintaining data quality. RAG models leverage 
medical ontologies and knowledge graphs to enhance NER accuracy, identifying and categorizing key clinical 
entities with high precision.
 Advanced applications include:
• Automated ICD-10 and CPT Coding: Extraction and assignment of appropriate diagnostic and procedural 
  codes from clinical notes
• Clinical Trial Matching: Identification of eligible patients by extracting relevant criteria from EHRs and 
  matching against trial protocols
• Adverse Event Detection: Automated identification and reporting of adverse drug reactions and medical 
  errors from clinical documentation
• Quality Measure Calculation: Extraction of data elements required for healthcare quality reporting and 
  performance metrics
These systems can automatically populate EHRs with diagnostic codes or flag potential drug interactions by 
cross-referencing patient data with up-to-date pharmacological databases, thereby reducing administrative bur- 
dens and minimizing errors in data entry.

2.3 Advanced diagnostic and treatment planning

RAG models support differential diagnosis and personalized treatment planning by integrating multimodal data, 
including patient histories, imaging reports, genomic profiles, and population health data. The systems provide 
clinicians with evidence-based recommendations while maintaining transparency through source attribution.
 Clinical applications encompass:
• Differential Diagnosis Support: Generation of ranked differential diagnoses with supporting evidence from 
  medical literature
• Personalized Treatment Recommendations: Integration of patient-specific factors with current treatment 
  guidelines and emerging therapies
• Drug Dosing and Selection: Optimization of medication choices based on patient characteristics, drug 
  interactions, and efficacy data
• Prognostic Assessment: Risk stratification and outcome prediction using integrated clinical and research data
In oncology, for instance, a RAG-augmented LLM could retrieve the latest research on targeted therapies while 
correlating it with a patient’s genetic markers, tumor characteristics, and treatment history. Multi-turn dialog 
systems powered by RAG enable dynamic interactions, allowing clinicians to refine queries iteratively—such as

2.4 Multimodal applications in medical imaging and pathology

RAG extends its utility to visual data interpretation, bridging the gap between imaging analysis and textual 
medical knowledge. These systems integrate computer vision capabilities with medical literature retrieval to 
provide comprehensive diagnostic support.
 Key implementations include:
• Radiological Report Generation: Automated creation of structured radiology reports by combining image 
  analysis with relevant diagnostic criteria and case studies
• Pathology Image Analysis: Correlation of histopathological findings with literature on rare diseases and 
  diagnostic patterns
• Clinical Photography Assessment: Analysis of dermatological conditions, wound healing progress, and 
  surgical outcomes
• Medical Image Education: Generation of educational content by linking imaging findings to relevant medical 
  literature and case studies
In radiology, these models generate detailed descriptions of X-rays, CT scans, or MRIs by retrieving similar cases 
and current diagnostic criteria, aiding radiologists in detecting anomalies and reducing interpretation time. For 
pathology, RAG can correlate histopathology images with literature on rare diseases, improving diagnostic 
confidence and supporting subspecialty consultation. Multimodal QA systems further allow clinicians to submit 
image-based queries (e.g., “Identify this skin lesion”) and receive synthesized insights combining visual and 
textual evidence.

2.5 Clinical research and evidence synthesis

RAG models significantly enhance clinical research capabilities by automating literature reviews, protocol 
development, and data analysis workflows. These applications support evidence-based medicine and accelerate 
the translation of research findings into clinical practice.
 Research applications include:
• Systematic Literature Reviews: Automated identification, screening, and synthesis of relevant research 
  papers for meta-analyses
• Clinical Protocol Development: Generation of research protocols by integrating regulatory requirements with 
  best practices
• Grant Writing Support: Assistance in literature review sections and methodology development for research 
  proposals
• Biomarker Discovery: Integration of genomic, proteomic, and clinical data to identify potential therapeutic 
  targets

facilitating patient engagement. These tools ensure that patient education is both accurate and tailored to indi-
